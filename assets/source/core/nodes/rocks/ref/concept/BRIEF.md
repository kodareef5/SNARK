# Concept art: the rocks of a ruined city by a bay

You are painting the concept for the rocks of a survival game before they are modelled. The modeller builds to your pictures, so they decide what the rocks look like. Work only in this folder. Keep every picture the image tool gives you, full size, in `source/`, and every prompt you used in `prompts.md`. Python 3 with Pillow and NumPy is installed if you want to assemble sheets.

## What the game is and what rocks do in it

A survival game in the tradition of the big open-world dinosaur survival games: a player arrives with nothing, punches and picks at the world for stone, flint, wood and fibre, builds a hut, then a house, then a fort. Rocks are among the first things a player looks for: struck with a stone pick they give stone and flint; a particular darker rock gives metal ore, and finding it is a small triumph a player learns to spot from far off. Rocks are also what a player hides behind, climbs onto and runs round, and most of what fills a beach or a hillside. A rock is struck until it is used up; then it breaks, and what is left stands there until the rock grows back.

The world is a ruined, overgrown subtropical city by a bay, a long time after its people left: hot coast, palms and strangler figs and banyans with hanging roots, mangroves at the water, sea grape and palmetto, vines over everything, white sand, shallow turquoise water. The painted map of it is the first picture you are given: look at it to know the place.

## The three kinds of rock

1. **Pale coastal limestone.** The bedrock of this coast: oolitic and coral limestone, cream to buff to pale warm grey. It is porous and pitted all over, with solution holes from fingertip to fist size, sharp, jagged little pinnacles on its weathered tops and smoother faces lower down where sand has scoured it; bedding planes and cross-bedding run through it in shallow ledges and grooves; fossil shell and coral fragments show where it is broken. Exposed tops darken to a grey to blackish weathering crust, fresh breaks are pale cream. It comes as **boulders** (1.5 to 3.5 m across, 1 to 2 m tall: as big as a car at most, small enough that a player can jump onto most) and as **low outcrops** (ledges of bedded rock breaking the ground, 2 to 5 m across and knee to waist high). Some are **half sunk in sand**, with a band of sand-polished stone at the sand line. Some are **gripped by the roots of a strangler fig**: roots poured over the rock and gripping it, the tree itself out of the picture or a stump of it.
2. **The ore rock.** A darker, harder, denser stone that pushes up among the limestone: smooth-skinned and massive where the limestone is pitted and porous, breaking in clean sharp faces where the limestone crumbles, dark grey-green to blue-black, with veins and seams of brassy, metallic ore that catch the sun, and rust stains bleeding down from them. A player who has seen one must be able to pick out the next from 100 m across a hillside: by its dark colour among the pale rocks, by its shape and by the glint of its veins. It must not glow, sparkle like a crystal or look like a marker: it is a rock that rewards a good eye.
3. **Rubble.** The broken buildings of the city, here a place to quarry stone: heaps of broken reinforced concrete slabs with rusted rebar bent out of their edges, chunks of fallen wall with the stucco still on one face and paint the sun has faded to chalky pink, mint, aqua or pale yellow, broken cinder block, cut coral-stone blocks, a fallen column drum. Each heap is a single mass about the size of a boulder (2 to 4 m across, 1 to 2 m high), sitting on the ground, partly crusted with sand and grime and a few weeds in its cracks.

Each kind has several individuals, not one shape turned round: a shore of them must not look like copies.

## What is left when one is used up

A rock struck until it is used up breaks: what stays is its broken base, the lower part of that same rock with fresh pale fracture faces on top and a scatter of fallen fragments round it, low enough to step over or onto. Rubble is quarried down to a low flattened heap of smaller broken pieces.

## The sizes and the rules

A person is about 1.8 m tall: put one in the wide pictures for scale (a plainly dressed survivor, tiny in the frame, no face detail). Every rock rests on the ground as a solid thing: it grips the ground, it is not perched. Nothing overhangs far: a player must be able to walk round any of them and stand on top of most. Paint stone as stone: real volumes with strata and fractures that say which stone it is, real shadow in the pits and under ledges, not a blob with a noisy texture.

## What to paint

1. `shore.png`: the hero picture. A curve of white sand beach by the bay at mid afternoon: limestone boulders and a low outcrop at the back of the beach, one half sunk in the sand near the water, one gripped by fig roots under the trees, sea grape and palms behind; seen from where a player stands, at eye height about 10 m from the nearest rock.
2. `hillside.png`: a hillside of tropical hardwood hammock and palms with limestone outcrops and boulders, and two or three ore rocks among them, seen from about 80 m across a clearing. This picture decides whether the ore rock reads from far off.
3. `ruins.png`: a street of the ruined city taken back by the forest, with rubble heaps along it, seen from about 10 m.
4. `limestone.png`: a sheet of the limestone individuals alone on plain sand in one light, at a three quarter view: at least four boulders of clearly different shapes, two low outcrops, the half sunk one and the root-gripped one.
5. `ore.png`: a sheet of at least three ore rocks alone, in the same light, beside one limestone boulder for comparison.
6. `rubble.png`: a sheet of at least three rubble heaps alone, in the same light.
7. `used_up.png`: a sheet of what is left: a limestone boulder's broken base, an ore rock's broken base and a quarried rubble heap, each beside the whole one it came from.
8. `surfaces.png`: four close-ups, each about a metre across, of the surfaces the modeller must reproduce: weathered limestone (pits, pinnacles, crust, a bedding groove), fresh limestone fracture (pale, with fossil fragments), the ore rock with a vein and its rust stain, and a broken concrete edge with rebar beside a faded painted stucco face.
9. `flat.png`: the shore and the hillside again as an untextured, flat-shaded low-poly game would draw them far away: every rock a few flat-coloured faces, lighter on the sunlit tops, darker on the shadowed sides and in the crust, so that limestone, ore rock and rubble are still told apart by colour and shape alone.

Paint it as a game's production concept art: realistic stone, clear forms, strong light and shadow, nothing blurred or muddy, the kind of picture an art director would pin up and say "build that". The building tiers of this same game were painted the same way; the second picture you are given, the stone house, is the bar for finish and clarity (not for subject). Do not imitate any existing game's art; do not use, search for or download pictures of any game or any other source.

Argue with your own pictures and paint again until each is right: if a rock looks like a lump of clay, like a cartoon, like a sponge, too regular, too smooth, too noisy, too much like its neighbour, or perched on the ground, paint it again. The image tool can edit and extend its own pictures: use your accepted pictures as references for the next, so every picture shows the same rocks.

Then write `NOTES.md`: for each picture what it shows and the prompt it came from, the colours of each kind as sRGB values (limestone body, crust, fresh fracture, ore stone, vein, rust, concrete, rebar, the paints), and a short list of what the modeller must not miss, with what you would change if you painted them again.
