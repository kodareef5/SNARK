# Palms — construction and material direction

These palms belong to a drowned subtropical city: royal palms preserve fragments of civic order; coconuts lean into the light and water around it. Their shared family resemblance is a thin stem supporting a crown of separate feather fronds. The royal is a pale column with a green neck and a lifted crown. The coconut is a warm, bent line with a wider, hanging crown.

The paintings establish shape, material character and composition. The dimensions below are the build specification; do not derive exact metres from painted perspective. All dimensions are in metres unless noted. A standing adult is 1.8 m; the near-view camera is 1.7 m above the road. Heights for royals mean ground to the highest living tip. Coconut trunk lengths follow the curved centreline from ground to the crown attachment.

## Royal palm

### Stem and foot

Mature overall height is 14–20 m. The deliberately young R4 is 10.5 m. Keep the stem almost straight: mature axis lean 0–6 degrees, with a slow bend rather than a mechanical tilt at the foot. A 20 m tree may displace its crown centre about 0.2–1.5 m horizontally. Add small variation in a second horizontal axis so its centreline is not confined to a perfectly flat plane.

Use this diameter profile for a typical mature tree. Here `t` is distance along the grey stem divided by its total grey-stem length; the green crownshaft is excluded. Blend all changes smoothly.

| Position | Nominal diameter | Permitted individual range |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ground contact | 0.60 | 0.54–0.65 |
| 0.5 m above ground | 0.52 | 0.47–0.56 |
| Breast height, 1.3 m | 0.50 | 0.45–0.53 |
| Lower stem, t ≈ 0.28 | 0.55 | 0.51–0.58 |
| Broad, shallow swell, t ≈ 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.56–0.62 |
| t ≈ 0.65 | 0.48 | 0.43–0.52 |
| Immediately below crownshaft | 0.34 | 0.30–0.38 |

The swell is a long change of volume over several metres, not a bottle-shaped knob. Young trees use a narrower profile: approximately 0.40 at the foot, 0.34 at breast height, 0.40 at the swell and 0.26 under the shaft. Slightly oval sections are acceptable; avoid a regular faceted cylinder at arm's length.

The foot broadens gently during its lowest 0.20–0.30 m. Fine roots, mostly 2–10 mm across, form a dense collar embedded in pavement cracks. Keep the root mat within about 0.10 m of the trunk edge, normally under 0.80 m overall diameter, and under 0.06 m above the ground. Most roots disappear immediately into dirt. This is a contact detail, not a projecting obstacle.

### Crownshaft and crown

The glossy green shaft is 1.5–2.0 m long, approximately 0.30–0.38 m across. Show a few smooth overlapping sheath boundaries. It ends the grey stem cleanly. Frond petioles emerge around its upper end; leaves do not sprout at intervals down its entire length. Old lower fronds bend down past the shaft before detaching.

Build 12–18 live fronds for the ordinary adult presets, including 1–2 upright emerging spears. A stressed extension may have only 8–10 live fronds and a conspicuous gap. Expanded adult fronds measure 3.0–4.0 m along their midribs; young fronds 2.8–3.2 m. Crown width usually falls between 6 and 8 m. Give each frond its own arch, twist, age and leaflet breaks.

Distribute successive attachment azimuths by roughly 137.5 degrees with small irregular offsets, then vary the heights within the crown hub. This is a procedural starting pattern, not a visible spiral staircase. Young spears start 65–85 degrees above horizontal. Main crown fronds leave at 20–55 degrees, flatten through the middle, then drop to tip tangents of roughly 35–70 degrees below horizontal. The lowest 2–4 fronds leave nearly horizontally or slightly down; their tips finish 1.0–2.5 m below attachment. Retain open sectors between arches from every viewing direction.

The bare proximal petiole occupies about 12–18% of an expanded frond. Maximum feather width is around 35–50% of its length. Narrow leaflets grow on both sides; shorten them progressively over the last third to a fine midrib tip. For the close model, use approximately 65–100 leaflets per side, 0.25–0.65 m long in the broad middle region and about 0.015–0.035 m wide. These are art targets, with varied spacing and small grouped rotations. Do not form a perfectly flat comb. Some leaflets twist above and below the main plane, giving the royal a fuller feather profile than the coconut.

Healthy royals carry no dead skirt. Use 0–1 retained dead frond normally, up to 2 on a stressed individual. Keep the crownshaft visible. Yellow living lower fronds and dead brown fronds are separate states.

## Coconut palm

### Stem and foot

Trunk length is 9–14 m along the curve. Breast-height diameter is 0.30–0.40 m, usually 0.35. At approximately 60% of trunk length use 0.29–0.34; under the crown use 0.25–0.31. The basal swelling reaches 0.60–0.80 m across but contracts to the ordinary stem within the first 0.5–0.7 m. There is no green royal-style crownshaft.

Keep the lowest harvest zone reasonably upright and close to the post collider. Introduce most of the outward sweep above about 1.3 m. A 15–40 degree middle-trunk lean recovers toward a terminal section within roughly 5–20 degrees of vertical. Whole-trunk displacement can be 2–5 m. A kink changes tangent over 0.4–0.8 m rather than making a sharp elbow; an S-bend has two long, unequal curves. Add a smaller sideways component so a quarter-turn still reveals a bend.

The 0.60–0.80 m foot is wrapped in a mat of thin roots pressed into sand and concrete cracks. Limit the visible mat to 0.10 m beyond the swollen base on each side: 0.80–1.00 m overall diameter at most, under 0.08 m high. No root arches or buttresses. The compact basal swelling is part of the solid trunk; exposed fibres lie against the terrain.

### Crown, fruit and dead material

Use 13–22 live fronds, including 1–2 central spears, 4–5 m long along the midrib. Broad living crowns span 8–10 m; sparse damaged crowns can be narrower. Some long fronds must project nearly horizontally to retain the width, while others curl well below the crown hub. Keep the crown looser and more asymmetric than the royal, with large openings between individual arches.

Attach thick petiole bases around a short hub, roughly 0.4–0.6 m tall. Interleave radial directions and vertical tiers instead of making a single flat star. Main petioles leave at 10–45 degrees above horizontal; centre spears at 60–85 degrees. Midribs flatten before their distal third rolls downward. Mature tips finish 0.8–2.0 m below attachment; oldest living tips can finish 2–3 m below. Use 15–20% bare proximal petiole, a subtle folded V-section and a stronger midrib than the royal.

Leaflets hang from both sides of the midrib, approximately 75–110 per side in the close asset, mostly 0.40–0.85 m long and 0.025–0.05 m wide in the broad region. Slightly alternate their roots. Broadest feather width occurs before the midpoint; both sides taper toward a long narrow tip. Leaflets drape 20–60 degrees below the local frond plane, with a few torn or folded ends. The side study describes a curved three-dimensional leaf, not a rigid flat card.

Use 0–3 dead hanging fronds, typically 1–2. Their stout brown midribs remain attached, with folded, broken straw leaflets hanging close to the upper trunk. Do not make a continuous thatch skirt. Give fruiting trees 1–3 tucked clusters, 4–12 visible coconuts altogether, roughly 0.20–0.30 m long each. Mix green and yellow fruit with occasional brown mature husks. Add 1–3 fine branched ochre flower stalks below the petiole bases; these are close-range accents, not long branches.

For procedural curves, author 5–7 centreline control points, smooth them, measure the resulting arc length and rescale to the selected trunk length. Do not set vertical height equal to curved length. Use roughness about 0.65–0.85 on trunks, 0.22–0.35 on the crownshaft, 0.35–0.55 on live fronds and 0.75–0.90 on dead material. These are starting values for a conventional 0–1 roughness shader. Coconut rings vary roughly 0.07–0.18 m apart, with compressed bands around bends; royal scars are quieter, roughly 0.15–0.30 m apart. Vary spacing and wear rather than drawing identical rings. Keep the royal almost smooth in silhouette. Coconut relief should be millimetres, not centimetre-deep corrugation.

Allow roughly ±8% value and modest hue variation between individuals. Couple colour to age: newest leaves at the top, deeper mature outer fronds, yellowing at the oldest tier. Vary damage by whole leaf and small leaflet groups, not white noise across the entire crown.

## Harvested stumps

Keep the original tree's basal centreline, diameter, root collar and terrain contact. Typical remaining cut height is 0.50–0.80 m: royal sheet example 0.65; coconut 0.55. Those heights describe the main cut surface; occasional torn tips can stand 0.03–0.12 m above it.

Build an uneven crown of overlapping stone-hatchet facets with one higher torn side. The exposed interior is a field of short fibre ends and longer axial splinters, with a somewhat denser outer band. There are **no annual growth rings**, spiral discs or broad oak-like grain. Some fibres bend over the edge and some protrude upright. Avoid the perfectly planar face of a saw cut. Keep a few narrow fibrous chips and fallen frond pieces as separate optional debris, never covering the stump's contact or harvest access.

## Cards, rotation and collision

Each expanded frond is a separate narrow mesh strip carrying cut-out leaflets. Give the midrib real curvature with enough longitudinal segments to preserve the arch, a small V-fold and occasional twist. A practical close starting point is 6–10 longitudinal sections per frond, adjusted for curvature. Keep a visible midrib/petiole through gaps. Use double-sided foliage shading with coherent normals and restrained transmission so the undersides do not become black or glowing yellow sheets.

Do not build the entire crown as a camera-facing plane. Distribute fronds around the full hub and test front, side, rear and below. Missing sectors should tell a life history while leaving enough fronds to read from other rotations. Use a fixed seed for each individual, and vary stem, leaf ages, crown gaps and scars together. Uniform placement scales of about 0.90–1.10 are safe within the stated ranges; use the dedicated young preset for a substantially younger tree.

The trunk remains the simple solid harvest post. Keep the base and lower striking zone close to that collision shape; do not expand it to encompass the distant crown or the entire lateral sweep. The visual root fibres must lie nearly flush to the ground, with no raised scenery that a player appears to walk through. Stumps retain the same root origin and compact solid base. Final collision dimensions should be checked in the game's actual controller, especially on slopes and around coconut bends.

## Reading at 3 m and 500 m

| Species | At 3 m | At 500 m |
|---|---|---|
| Royal | Smooth pale column, long shallow swell, quiet pale rings, small scar and tight root contact. Looking up reveals a glossy green neck and lifted feather arches. | A light nearly straight line beneath a compact, elevated dark star. Keep some green shaft separation if pixels allow. Crown narrower and more upright than coconut. |
| Coconut | Warm ringed narrow stem, low swelling, fine fibres and a clear bend. Looking up reveals petiole bases, fruit, old stalks and loose drooping leaves. | A bent brown-grey line beneath a wider drooping star. Curve, offset crown and one optional dark hanging blade survive after fruit and bark disappear. |

For a distant tree about 20–60 pixels tall, protect the stem and 5–8 visible crown projections before any texture. Preserve several wedges of background between projections; a solid round canopy fails. At that size the stem may be only 1–2 pixels wide. Fade rings, leaflets, fruit and roots before they become flicker. Keep crown size and centre position consistent across detail levels. Judge screen size in the actual field of view; 500 m alone does not determine pixels.

The flat grove uses a few stem faces and 6–9 radial bent blades per palm, with only some blades visible from a given angle. Use 2–3 solid crown tones: underside #283C28, middle #4C6134, sunward #83964B, with sparse fresh tips #A0AD52 and old straw #A78A4D. These are intentionally shaded distant-palette targets, separate from the unlit material colours above. Broad crown blades taper to points; do not replace them with undifferentiated triangles piled into a ball.

Place dark green crown stars against warm ochre/sage ground, pale broken concrete and cool teal water. Let a few sunward tips catch light without turning the whole hillside chartreuse. Atmosphere gradually reduces contrast and warms/cools with the environment; it must not erase every thin stem at once. Keep occasional isolated crowns against the sky and the sea as readable anchors.

On the boulevard, use interrupted runs with approximately 7–10 m old planting spacing, removed trees, uneven survivors and younger arrivals. On sand, use irregular groups of 2–7 with variable 4–12 m gaps and larger clearings. These are distribution starting points, not a grid. Mix tall royal punctuation with lower, offset coconut crowns; break up repetition through azimuth, height, crown fullness and empty ground. Preserve walkable open space beneath the canopy.

The final acceptance test is a grove viewed at player height and again at a few dozen pixels per tree: a pale straight royal and a bent coconut should remain distinguishable before their materials load. The paintings guide that test; they are not a substitute for checking the scripted meshes and foliage cards in engine.
