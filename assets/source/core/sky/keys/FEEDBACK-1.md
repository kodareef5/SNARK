Keep all of that. Five things are short.

1. **Noon lost its ceiling, and that was my measure's fault.** Its grade could only brighten or darken the one point toward the sun, so a zenith 23° from the noon sun could not be made deeper than physics. I have changed `measure.py`: the glow now weighs every point by its nearness to the light, and the lift toward the horizon reaches higher, as a real grade's would. Run it again: noon now fits at 3.4 as it stands, with room to spare. Bring back the style sheet's deep mineral blue overhead, and let the upper sky of morning, noon and afternoon be a more saturated blue at the same brightness: 30° up away from the sun near (105, 185, 245) rather than (140, 183, 228), still as bright as (150, 190, 230).
2. **The afternoon toward the sun is a grey-white wash.** Hazier than noon, yes, but warm and luminous: a gold-white haze round the sun and low toward it, the blue clear above.
3. **The sea is mud at the low hours.** At dawn, the golden hour, sunset and dusk it is a dark brown-grey band under the brightest sky of the day. A calm sea seen from a hill takes the sky's colour, more so toward the horizon where it reflects almost all of it: toward the low sun a warm sheen with a glittering path straight under the disc, away from it the cooler blue and rose of that side's sky, a little darker than the sky above. Noon's and morning's deep blue sea is right; keep it.
4. **The night is darker than it needs to be.** Physics at the night's exposure gives a zenith near (43, 58, 81); yours is (12, 24, 44). A player reads the night by the land's shapes against the sky, so the sky is what makes it readable: lift it to a luminous deep blue, roughly (20, 36, 68) overhead to (60, 90, 130) at the horizon, with a wider soft glow round the moon. Leave the land and the card where they are.
