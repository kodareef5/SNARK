# Thatch building tier: concept art, round 1 (design directions)

You are the concept artist for the cheapest building tier of a survival game. Players build bases out of square pieces by the thousand: foundations, walls, doorways, doors and ceilings. This tier is the first shelter a player ever builds, minutes after waking on a beach with nothing: bundled reed or long grass thatch lashed to a frame of hand-cut poles. Light, uneven, quick to put up, and it looks it. It must also be a place you would want to sleep in: warm, golden, handmade, a little wild.

Anyone who has played a dinosaur survival game knows this tier at a glance as "straw on a frame of rough sticks". Ours must read as that instantly and still be clearly our own design.

A 3D artist will build exactly what you paint, piece by piece, so paint things that can be built, at the sizes given below. This round is about choosing a design direction; later rounds will develop the chosen one into a full set of views.

Work only in the current folder. Take as long as the job needs. Generate many pictures, look at each critically, throw away the weak ones, and push the good ones further. Keep only what you would show an art director.

## The pieces, and the rules every design must obey

Sizes are exact. The house is boxy because the game is: there is no pitched roof in this set.

- **Foundation**: a 3 m by 3 m platform, 0.375 m tall, standing on the ground. Its top is the floor. Foundations are laid edge to edge, so a floor of four is a 6 m square, and its top must run flat and unbroken across the joins.
- **Wall**: 3 m long, 3 m tall, 0.2 m thick, standing on the edge of a foundation. Walls stand edge to edge in long runs and meet at corners, so the thatch must carry on from one wall into the next without a gap. A few centimetres of shaggy stems proud of the face is fine and good; nothing may stick out further than about 10 cm. Both faces are seen: the outside and the inside of the house.
- **Doorframe**: a wall with a doorway 1.4 m wide and 2.31 m tall in its middle, its sill at floor level. Whatever frames the opening stays out of the opening.
- **Door**: 1.4 m wide, 2.31 m tall, 8 cm thick, hung in the doorway on one side; it swings open by a quarter turn.
- **Ceiling**: a 3 m by 3 m slab, 0.25 m thick, whose top is level with the top of the walls. A house of four foundations has four ceilings as its roof, laid edge to edge, and their tops are also the floor of a storey above. So the roof is flat on top; its edges, seen from the ground, are the eaves. The edge of a ceiling may hang out over the wall below it by up to about 30 cm and droop, but its top stays flat and walkable.

The standard picture is a small house: a 2 by 2 floor of foundations (6 m square), walls all round with one doorway (door open, showing the dark interior), and a roof of four ceilings. About 6 m by 6 m by 3.4 m tall.

## What excellent looks like

Thatch is a mass of long stems, and the shape must say so before any texture does:

- **Thickness you can see at every edge.** Thatch is 10 to 20 cm thick and soft. Where a wall ends, where the eave turns a corner, at the foot of a wall, the edge is a thick ragged mass of stems, never a thin straight line.
- **A ragged foot.** Stems end at different lengths; the bottom edge of every layer is uneven and splays a little, and casts a broken shadow.
- **Sag and bulge between ties.** Thatch is held by poles or rods lashed across it; between them it bulges and sags, at them it is pinched in.
- **Hollows that go dark**, under every overhang and between clumps.
- **Tips bleached by the sun**, paler and greyer than the golden body; older or more exposed parts a little silvered.
- **Strays**: the odd stem or small tuft sticking out.
- **Clumps that differ**: bundles of different widths and fullness, leaning a little, never in ruler-straight courses, never all the same.
- **A frame of hand-cut poles** that are not straight and not the same: different thicknesses, a gentle bow, bark in patches, axe-cut ends, lashed at every joint with twisted grass cord or bark strip.
- **Each piece is its own made thing.** A wall, a doorframe, a roof, a door and a foundation are built differently and look it. A roof sheds water. A door is carried on a light frame of its own. A foundation holds the ground back.

What we do not want: anything that looks like a pattern printed on a box. Same-width bundles cut square. Straight outlines. A flat roof wearing the wall's texture. Brickwork-regular courses. Siding.

**Style.** A still from a finished, high-end modern survival game running at maximum settings: realistic materials and light, with shapes chunky and readable enough for a game where you see hundreds of pieces at once. Not a cartoon, not a documentary photograph of a real village. Colour natural and warm: straw from pale cream tips to golden body to olive-brown hollows; poles warm brown with grey weathering; cord pale tan. Nothing green or orange in the thatch itself.

**Light.** Real light: warm sun low in the afternoon, long soft shadows, sky light in the shadows, the interior dark through the doorway. The setting is a grassy clearing at the edge of a tropical forest near a coast, kept quiet so the building is the subject.

## This round: four design directions

Paint the standard small house in **four clearly different design directions**, each from the same kind of view: a three-quarter view from where a player stands, about 9 m away at eye height (1.7 m), sun from over the viewer's shoulder at an angle so the faces show their relief. Each direction is one coherent idea of how this tier is built. Some starting points; mix them, or find better ones:

- Walls of long vertical stems in two tall tiers, the upper tier's ragged foot hanging over the lower one at mid-height, held by horizontal rods lashed across the face.
- Walls of overlapping horizontal courses of tied bundles, like a thatched roof turned upright, each course's foot ragged and uneven, sagging between ties.
- Walls of thick grass panels woven or bound between upright poles, with a shaggy top and foot.
- A heavier, older, weathered look with silvered thatch and darker poles.

For every direction, also show how the roof edge (eave), the foundation and the doorway are made. Make each house a place you would want to live in.

Then, for the strongest **two** directions, paint a second view from inside the house, looking at the inside face of a wall and up at the underside of the roof, lit by the sun through the doorway.
