## A — Long Skirts

Two tall, overlapping layers of long vertical reeds are clamped to a sparse sapling frame by crooked horizontal rods and grass ties. Broad bellies gather beneath the bindings; the upper skirt ends in one deep, broken shadow above the lower skirt. The interior exposes the retaining rods and calmer backs of those layers. The ceiling uses horizontal reed packs over small parallel branch joists, with a compressed walking skin and a short turned-down fringe at exposed eaves. The foundation is a shallow pole crib retaining earth beneath a flat reed floor. Slender lashed jambs and a lintel bind back the thick doorway reveals; the separate door has vertical reed infill, small crossbars and a diagonal twig brace. **Tier read:** long grass, very few sticks, obvious hand assembly. **Weaknesses:** the long fringe can become a repetitive horizontal stripe across large bases; local bundle variation must survive without changing the module boundary. Its sparse frame also emphasizes how provisional this shelter is.

## B — Shingled Sheaves

Four overlapping courses of short reed sheaves are tied near their heads to small rods, leaving uneven pale feet and dark sheltered overlaps. The inside shows the binding rows behind a calmer vertical reed retaining layer, all within the wall thickness. Cross-laid horizontal roof packs sit on a light branch frame with narrow retaining switches; their outer ends form the short ragged drip fringe. Horizontal reed fascines and short stakes face the earth-filled foundation, whose bound reed floor remains level. Forked jamb heads cradle the doorway lintel outside the opening; the thin door carries a compact reed mat on its own small frame and crossbars. **Tier read:** an unmistakable soft thatch silhouette and a direct explanation of how each handful is attached. **Weaknesses:** four courses create more repeated edges and visual noise across hundreds of pieces; the hems must stay shallow and irregular to avoid becoming siding. More separate bundles also imply more assembly work than A.

## C — Bound Soft Panels

Full-height grass infill is compressed into soft panels by three sparse paired-cord rows tied through to slender backing rods. The few upright saplings establish the frame; grass continues behind them, swelling gently between bindings and ending in a thick loose foot. A stitched horizontal reed mattress on small branch joists forms the flat ceiling, with a short brush-like eave. The foundation retains earth behind diagonal twig wattle under a compact reed walking mat. Simple lashed poles frame the doorway; its independent door is a finer stitched reed mat on a diagonally braced twig frame. **Tier read:** abundant raw grass, visible cord pressure and a simple tied skeleton. **Weaknesses:** the relatively continuous surface can read as upholstered panels or combed hair unless stem breaks, hollows and uneven compression remain prominent. Cord spacing is still the most orderly of the four directions, and the wattle foundation suggests more craft time.

## D — Weathered Wind Mats

Long reeds lie in broad oblique mats, compressed by a waist rod and sparse diagonal switches. Silvered exposed ends, darker weathered saplings and warmer protected grass give it age without decay. The roof is a compact horizontal reed pack with a short wind-combed edge; it remains level rather than following the wall diagonals. Darker crib poles and recessed horizontal reed packs retain the foundation earth beneath its flat reed mat. Slender jambs and a rough lintel keep the opening clear; the separate light door repeats the diagonal reed infill on a braced twig frame. **Tier read:** crude bindings, wind-loosened grass and rough branches. **Weaknesses:** the diagonal emphasis can flatten into a chevron surface pattern, and the grey palette is less immediately golden and welcoming. It reads as an enduring, weathered version of the starter tier more readily than a shelter made minutes ago.

## Selection

**Advance A as the primary direction.** One large overlap gives it a strong silhouette at gameplay distance while preserving the feeling of long stems. Its few exposed rods explain assembly without overwhelming the grass. The plain retaining crib, thin braced door and horizontal roof packs give each piece its own construction language. The inside remains warm and simple.

**Keep B as the alternate.** Its overlapping sheaves have the richest small shadows and strongest weather-shedding wall logic. The interior demonstrates how that busy exterior can still produce a calm room. It needs more restraint in repetition than A. C and D remain useful distinct alternatives, rather than additional views of A.

## Shared construction dimensions

These are the modeling dimensions for all four directions. The images are perspective design studies; use these numerical dimensions when blocking out the pieces.

| Piece / datum | Fixed dimension and assembly |
| --- | --- |
| Foundation | 3 × 3 × 0.375 m; four form a 6 × 6 m floor. Retaining faces below floor level. Tops meet flush. |
| Wall | 3 × 3 × 0.2 m, measured from foundation top. Thick reeds continue at seams and corners. Loose stems project no more than 0.10 m. |
| Doorframe | Same wall envelope; centered 1.4 × 2.31 m clear opening. Each side shoulder is 0.8 m wide, including its jamb. No frame or raised sill enters the opening. |
| Door | 1.4 × 2.31 × 0.08 m, including its small frame; fiber-loop hinges on one side; 90° swing. |
| Ceiling | 3 × 3 × 0.25 m, including frame and reed skin. Four panels meet flush. Top at wall-top height; all top surfaces level and walkable. |
| Vertical datums | Ground 0; foundation top 0.375 m; ceiling underside 3.125 m; wall and ceiling top 3.375 m. Interior clear height below slab 2.75 m. |
| Eave | Short soft fringe on exposed perimeter edges, approximately 0.20–0.25 m projection, below the flat top datum; never beyond the 0.30 m allowance. Internal ceiling edges butt flush. |
| Poles | Main saplings approximately 0.08–0.12 m diameter, up to 0.14 m at corners; smaller binding rods and switches. Keep the whole wall assembly inside its envelope. |

The roofs use horizontal compressed reed skins and turned-down exposed ends to suggest shedding water. They are makeshift flat game roofs, not a claim of real-world waterproof construction. No pitched cap or extra roof thickness is implied. Model the perimeter fringe separately from mating edges so adjoining ceiling tops stay unbroken.
